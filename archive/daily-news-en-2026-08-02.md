## 🌊 Story 1: Trump Pulls Back From the Brink — Cancels Iran Strikes, Says a Deal Is Within Reach
**Source:** BBC News | **Reporters:** James Chater & Joe Inwood

For a few nerve-wracking hours, the world held its breath. President Donald Trump has announced he's cancelling what would have been one of the most intense bombing campaigns against Iran since this war began in February — but only on the condition that a deal is struck "rapidly." In a Saturday post on Truth Social, Trump claimed that Iran and other Middle Eastern countries had actually asked Washington to hold off, saying the "perimeters" of a deal had been agreed.

Here's the context that makes this so dizzying. Just a day earlier, during a cabinet meeting at Camp David, Trump was caught on microphone telling aides — with reporters still in the room — "We'll be hitting them very hard. At some point, they're going to say, 'We just can't take it anymore.'" US media reported that Washington and Israel were planning devastating strikes on Iranian energy infrastructure. The US government had even warned Americans across the Middle East to be ready to evacuate. And then, almost as suddenly as the rhetoric escalated, Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed Bin Salman got on the phone with Trump, emphasising "the necessity of prioritising dialogue." The Saudi intervention appears to have been pivotal.

Now, here's why you should take all of this with a grain of salt. This isn't the first time Trump has issued apocalyptic warnings only to pivot to diplomacy. Back in April, he warned that "a whole civilisation will die tonight" if Iran didn't come to the table — and while that threat did produce historic face-to-face talks in Pakistan led by Vice President JD Vance, those talks collapsed without a deal. Iran's acting Defence Minister, Majid Ibn al-Reza, said Sunday that Tehran views every threat as "real and credible" and will "neither be caught off guard nor remain passive." Meanwhile, Gulf allies like Bahrain, Jordan, and the UAE are deeply nervous — their critical infrastructure, including water desalination plants, sits well within range of Iranian missiles even if the American mainland doesn't. And with roughly 60% of Americans disapproving of this war and midterm elections looming, Trump knows his international gambles have domestic consequences he can't ignore.

## 🌏 Story 2: Europe Burns — Italy Puts Nearly Every Major City on Highest Heat Alert as Wildfires Rage
**Source:** BBC News | **Reporter:** Jaroslav Lukiv

If you're in Europe right now, you're probably not thinking about geopolitics — you're just trying to survive the heat. Italy has placed 19 of its 27 major cities on the highest "red" alert, and by Monday that number jumps to 25. We're talking Rome, Milan, Venice, Naples — essentially the entire country, from the Alps to Sicily, is baking under a subtropical anticyclone that's drifted up from North Africa and parked itself over the Mediterranean. Temperatures hit the mid-30s Celsius on Saturday, and health officials aren't just warning the usual vulnerable groups — they're telling *everyone*, including healthy and active people, to stay indoors between 11am and 6pm and drink plenty of water.

This isn't happening in isolation. Across the Mediterranean, firefighters in Greece, France, Portugal, and Spain are battling blazes that have consumed thousands of hectares. In France's Var region, 2,500 people were evacuated Friday night when a fire that had been contained for days suddenly reignited. In Spain, more than 200,000 hectares have burned so far in 2026 — roughly five times above the average for this point in the year. And over in the Western US, a separate heat dome is shattering temperature records and raising wildfire risks from California to the Rockies. Europe, remember, is the fastest-warming continent on Earth — heating up at twice the global average, according to the Copernicus Climate Change Service. These aren't anomalies anymore. They're the new normal, arriving earlier and hitting harder each year.

## 🌎 Story 3: Tragedy Over the Nazca Lines — Tourist Plane Crashes in Peru, All 13 On Board Killed
**Source:** BBC News | **Reporter:** Grace Eliza Goodwin

A sightseeing flight over one of the world's most mysterious archaeological wonders ended in catastrophe on Saturday. A Cessna Caravan C-208 carrying 11 tourists and 2 pilots went down near the Nazca Lines in southern Peru, killing everyone on board. The passengers included two Germans, two Spaniards, and seven Italians — a sobering reminder that these ancient desert geoglyphs, while breathtaking, have been the site of far too many aviation disasters.

The plane belonged to local airline Aerodiana and had departed from Pisco, Peru, around 1pm local time. Video from the scene shows firefighters spraying water at smoking wreckage in the Pueblo Viejo region. Police Major Jorge Andrade told reporters, "Given the severity of the accident, there are no survivors. We have recovered four bodies so far." The cause of the crash is still unknown. But here's the chilling part: this is not the first time a tourist plane has gone down over the Nazca Lines. In 2022, another small aircraft crashed during a similar flight, killing seven people. In 2010, six died in yet another accident. The flights are popular because they're really the only way to appreciate the full scale of the enormous animal-shaped drawings — some stretching hundreds of metres across the desert floor, carved by the Nazca culture around 1,600 years ago. But the frequency of these crashes raises serious questions about safety standards for the small-plane tourism industry that has grown up around this UNESCO World Heritage site.

## 🌍 Story 4: The Mountain Takes a Legend — Nirmal Purja Killed in Pakistan Avalanche
**Source:** BBC News | **Reporter:** Tinshui Yeung

The mountaineering world is in mourning today. Nirmal "Nimsdai" Purja — the British-Nepali climber who shattered records by scaling all 14 of the world's 8,000-metre peaks in just 189 days — has been confirmed dead at age 43, killed alongside at least nine other climbers in an avalanche on Broad Peak in Pakistan's Karakoram range. His company, Elite Expeditions, announced the news with "profound sadness and immense heartbreak." His brother Kamal wrote: "We will miss you every single day."

If you've seen the Netflix documentary *14 Peaks: Nothing Is Impossible*, you know exactly who this man was. A former Gurkha who served 16 years in the British military — 10 of those in special forces — Purja became a global icon of human endurance. Prince William called him a man who "served with distinction" before "becoming one of the greatest mountaineers." Nepal's prime minister said his death had "shocked us." And here's what makes it especially heartbreaking: in his very last post on X, published just Monday, Purja wrote that climbing Broad Peak "wasn't planned." He was already in Pakistan for Gasherbrum II, and realised that if he also summited Broad Peak, he'd be just one mountain away from becoming the first person in history to climb all 14 eight-thousanders — twice — without supplemental oxygen. "Broad Peak, I ask for nothing but safe passage up and back down," he wrote. "I take zero mountains for granted." Rescue teams have recovered three bodies so far — American climber Mallory Geis, Nadhira Ahmed Abdullah Al Harthy from Oman, and Pur Bahadur Gurung from Nepal — but the rest, including Purja, remain on the mountain. Broad Peak, at 8,047 metres, has claimed dozens of lives since its first ascent in 1957. It has claimed one of the greatest climbers who ever lived.

## 🌏 Story 5: "Spain Is Open" — How a Viral Social Media Surge Drew Thousands to Their Deaths in Ceuta
**Source:** BBC News | **Reporter:** Sarah Rainsford, Southern and Eastern Europe Correspondent

It started with a whisper on social media and ended with at least 67 bodies pulled from the sea. Tens of thousands of migrants — mostly young Moroccans — swam toward the Spanish exclave of Ceuta on Thursday, chasing viral posts that promised an easy path into Europe. Videos circulated online showed people in wetsuits and flippers shouting "Spain is Open!" and jumping into the water. The promises were false. Most of those who made it to shore were met with nothing — no food, no shelter, no onward route to mainland Europe — and were escorted back to Morocco by Spanish soldiers within hours. But 67 never made it back at all.

The political fallout has been swift and brutal. Italy's hard-right Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni was among the first to pounce, posting images of migrants running through Ceuta's streets and announcing she was suspending Italy's Schengen agreement with Spain, citing a security threat. Madrid is furious — pointing out, correctly, that Ceuta isn't even in the Schengen zone and that the crisis was contained. But with Meloni facing elections next year and losing ground to an even more virulently anti-immigrant party, this was a political gift she wasn't going to pass up. Nearly two dozen EU heads of state have now signed on to an emergency meeting of interior ministers, calling for the elimination of all "pull factors" to Europe. Spain's Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez has hit back, accusing some EU partners of "attacking" Spain rather than showing solidarity. Meanwhile, the global right is, as one Spanish journalist put it, "hyperventilating with happiness" — Trump has called it an "invasion," and Russia is gloating over the chaos. The investigation into who or what sparked the social media surge is ongoing, but for now, the only certainty is this: dozens of young people who set out chasing a vision they saw on their phones are being sent home in coffins.
