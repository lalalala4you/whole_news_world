## 🌏 Story 1: Trump Alleges China Hacked Voter Data in Primetime Address

**Source:** BBC News | **Reporters:** Bernd Debusmann Jr. & Anthony Zurcher

President Donald Trump delivered a half-hour primetime address from the White House on Thursday, accusing China of interfering in the 2020 presidential election and alleging "shocking vulnerabilities" in American voting systems — a claim that directly contradicts previous US intelligence assessments.

Speaking just three months ahead of the November midterm elections, Trump said he had declassified hundreds of intelligence files showing Beijing had tried to sway the 2020 election in Joe Biden's favour. He specifically accused China of the "illicit acquisition" of 220 million voter files and claimed voter data in 18 states was "bought, stolen, or hacked by China." The president did not, however, present evidence that any of this alleged data was used to alter voting outcomes.

The Chinese Embassy in Washington immediately pushed back, stating that Beijing "has never and will never interfere in the presidential elections." Meanwhile, Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer accused Trump of trying to sow doubt ahead of the midterms, while former Vice President Kamala Harris posted that Trump "wants you to lose confidence in our electoral system so you stay home this November." A 2021 National Intelligence Council report had previously found with "high confidence" that China did not interfere in the 2020 election.

> **Why it matters:** With Trump's approval rating at 37% and midterms looming, this speech sets the stage for a highly contentious election season — and revives debates about election security that have roiled American politics for years.

## 🌊 Story 2: Philippines Slams China's 'Racist' AI Monkey Video in South China Sea Row

**Source:** BBC News | **Reporter:** Kelly Ng

The Philippines has formally condemned what it called a "dehumanising and racist" AI-generated video posted by Chinese state media outlet *China Daily*, which depicts the Philippines as a timid monkey being strong-armed by the US and Japan to challenge Beijing in the South China Sea.

The video, which remains online on China Daily's Facebook page, shows a monkey in a Filipino shirt being shoved onto a karaoke stage on a boat by arms bearing American and Japanese flags. It is then flung into the sea and blasted by a water cannon — an unmistakable reference to the Chinese Coast Guard's repeated use of high-pressure water cannons against Philippine vessels in disputed waters. Manila's foreign ministry called the clip "contemptible propaganda" and Defence Secretary Gilbert Teodoro said it "exposes the moral and intellectual bankruptcy of China's propaganda machine."

The timing is particularly sensitive: this past week marked the 10th anniversary of the Permanent Court of Arbitration's landmark ruling in favour of the Philippines, which concluded that China's expansive claims in the South China Sea have no basis under international law — a ruling Beijing has consistently ignored. Tensions have escalated sharply in recent months, with confrontations involving swords, spears, and floating barriers.

> **Why it matters:** This AI-generated propaganda video marks a troubling new front in the South China Sea dispute — and risks inflaming already dangerously high tensions between two US allies and Beijing.

## 🔥 Story 3: Canada Wildfire Smoke Blankets US Cities, Air Quality Hits Hazardous Levels

**Source:** BBC News | **Reporters:** Chris Fawkes, Rachel Flynn & Nadine Yousif

A thick blanket of smoke from more than 800 active wildfires across Canada has triggered hazardous air quality alerts across major US cities, from Detroit and Chicago to New York, as the worst wildfire season in recent memory continues to intensify.

New York Governor Kathy Hochul described the situation as "a very serious health situation," with the iconic New York City skyline — including the Empire State Building and the Statue of Liberty — obscured by a thick orange-grey haze. The city activated emergency protocols, making cooling centres and KN95 masks available citywide. Outdoor events, including children's summer camps and concerts, have been cancelled, and beaches shuttered. Air quality in Detroit is currently the worst in the world, according to Swiss tracker IQAir.

The fires are concentrated in northwestern Ontario, where one First Nations chief reported her community had been "burnt to ashes." The smoke is expected to persist through the weekend, raising concerns it could drift into New Jersey, where Sunday's World Cup final is scheduled. Experts note that while wildfires are part of Canada's boreal forest lifecycle, they have become markedly more frequent since 2015, linked to climate-driven atmospheric drying.

> **Why it matters:** For the third consecutive year, Canadian wildfires are choking North American cities with toxic smoke — a stark, recurring reminder of how climate change is reshaping what "summer" means for millions of people.

## 👑 Story 4: Japan Revises Imperial Succession Law — But Women Still Barred From the Throne

**Source:** Kyodo News / The Straits Times

Japan's Parliament approved the first substantive revision to the 1947 Imperial House Law on Friday, addressing the shrinking imperial family while controversially leaving intact the male-only succession rule — despite polls showing 83% of the Japanese public supports allowing female emperors.

The revised law introduces two key changes: it permits the adoption of males aged 15 and over from 11 former branch families that lost their royal status under the US occupation after World War II, and it allows female imperial members to retain their royal status even after marrying commoners. Previously, women like former Princess Mako had to relinquish their titles upon marriage. Under the new system, male descendants of adopted members could potentially ascend the Chrysanthemum Throne — but a woman still cannot.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, Japan's first female premier, made the revision a legislative priority. However, she has faced sharp criticism from opposition lawmakers who argue the process lacked sufficient parliamentary deliberation and fell far short of what the public wants. The law will be reviewed every 30 years if necessary. With only three male heirs currently in the 16-member imperial family, the succession issue remains a ticking clock.

> **Why it matters:** Japan's monarchy is the world's oldest hereditary institution — and this partial reform, while historic, deepens the debate over whether tradition can survive without embracing equality in a modern democracy.

## 🌡️ Story 5: European Heatwave Sparks Frenzy for Chinese 'Cult' Air Conditioner

**Source:** BBC News | **Reporter:** Koh Ewe

As Western Europe endures its hottest June on record — with cities across Germany and France topping 40°C — a previously little-known Chinese appliance has become the summer's most coveted item: the Midea PortaSplit, a portable split air-conditioner that has sold out across the continent and is being resold at triple its retail price.

The craze centres on the PortaSplit's clever design: it features a lightweight outdoor unit that users can perch outside their windows themselves, bypassing strict European regulations and historic-preservation rules that normally ban drilling for external AC units. Fan communities have sprung up online, complete with a tracking website called MideaFinder, and owners describe it as a "game changer" and even a "cult." Chinese manufacturers TCL and Gree have also reported surging European sales — Gree told the BBC that many customers are first-time AC buyers.

But the boom has a darker side. Air conditioning already accounts for 7% of global electricity use and 2.7% of CO₂ emissions. Environmental advocates worry that as heatwaves become the new normal, Europe's AC adoption could create a vicious cycle of more energy consumption, more emissions, and hotter cities. France has already committed €80 million to installing cooling systems in schools and community centres.

> **Why it matters:** The Midea PortaSplit isn't just a gadget — it's a symbol of how Europe's relationship with air conditioning, and with climate adaptation itself, is being fundamentally rewritten by a warming planet.
